What your agents are worth, and why your financials can't see it yet

What this is

This briefing explains a shift we are building a company around. AI capability is becoming abundant and cheap. The scarce thing is proof: the ability to show what an agent's work is actually worth, in terms a CFO, an auditor, a regulator, or an insurer will accept. We call the value that exists but cannot yet be seen Dark Value. The page below lays out where it comes from, why the market is about to pay for making it visible, and how Applied Identities turns it into recognized, defensible value. Near the end there is a model you can run yourself. It is about a six-minute read.

Start with the insurers

The most revealing move in enterprise AI this year did not come from a model lab. It came from the insurance industry. Over the opening months of 2026, Berkshire Hathaway, Chubb, and Travelers won approval from state regulators to remove AI-related damages from standard commercial liability, and regulators signed off on more than four in five requests. The firms whose entire business is pricing risk looked at AI and decided, for now, not to price it. That deserves more attention than most product launches, because an underwriter is the market's final scorer. When the party that prices risk for a living steps back, it is saying it cannot yet see the risk clearly enough to put a number on it.

The thing the accounts can't see

A recent SemiAnalysis piece by Malcolm Spittler and Dylan Patel gives the underlying logic a name worth borrowing: Dark Output, the value AI produces that national accounts cannot see. When an agent drafts a document a lawyer used to bill for, the receipt simply vanishes, and the statistics can record falling output and even rising prices in the exact place where productivity climbed. SemiAnalysis sells a paid monitor for this, so we separate their commercial interest from the structural claim. The structural claim is not new. Robert Solow noticed in 1987 that the computer age was visible everywhere except the productivity statistics, and the productivity J-curve later explained why: a general-purpose technology demands heavy, invisible investment before its gains surface. Dark Output is the sharpest current statement of a forty-year-old problem.

Dark Value

The macro accounts are blind to AI's output for structural reasons. The enterprise income statement is blind for the same reasons. Across the 2026 surveys the pattern repeats: the value is real, it is felt at the desk, and it does not survive the trip to the income statement, because nothing in the deployment was built to make it survive.

That gap is Dark Value, and most companies are generating it and throwing it away. The strategic point follows: legibility, not capability, is the scarce input, and scarce inputs are where value accrues.

How value becomes real: it has to climb

Proof comes in strengths, not as a yes or no. A benchmark is a claim about software under test. A company's own report on its own agent is, from the outside, hard to tell apart from marketing. A production deployment a customer can inspect is stronger. An assessment an outside party will sign is stronger still. At the top sits a risk an insurer will underwrite, because a disinterested third party has examined the work, priced the failure, and agreed to carry it. The work itself does not change as it climbs. What changes is who, outside the company, will stand behind it. Legibility is manufactured by independence, the same way external audit superseded internal controls after the accounting scandals of the early 2000s. And the top of that ladder is becoming a live market right now. Independent analyses from Lockton Re with Armilla and from Gallagher Re find that standard liability was never built to model AI risk, the exposure lands on the company that deploys the agent, and coverage increasingly goes only to firms that can document their deployments and prove their controls work. The asset is not the policy. It is the verifiable record that makes the policy writable.

The part that compounds

Substitution is the smaller story. The larger one is new work, tasks no one would have paid a human to do, now done constantly because the cost collapsed. The same holds inside a single company. A specification says what an agent should do; continuous measurement records what it actually did, and the space between the two is not only error to correct. Some of it is value no one scoped, invisible until something is watching closely enough to catch it. Caught and verified, it can be promoted into the accountable record and recognized. A company that measures only against its original plan sees substitution. A company that measures continuously sees substitution plus the emergent share, and the emergent share is the part that compounds. A firm with a year of that record holds an asset a competitor starting today cannot reconstruct at any budget.

Why this is the company, and the investment

Capability is becoming a commodity. The ability to prove what an agent's work is worth is not, and three forces are converging on it at once. The insurance market is repricing AI risk and will write coverage only against evidence. The regulatory regimes, the EU AI Act, the NIST framework and its 2026 extension to autonomous agents, and ISO/IEC 42001, ask for the same specification, ongoing measurement, and external verifiability. And enterprises are discovering they cannot bank the value they already feel. Applied Identities builds the instrument that makes agent value legible, defensible, and ultimately insurable. The case is two steps. Our product creates real, recognized, compounding value for the companies that deploy it; that value is the market; and we capture a share of it. The client value is the evidence, and our value follows from it. That is what we are building, and it is what we are inviting you to back.
