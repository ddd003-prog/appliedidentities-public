Dark Value

What your AI agents are worth, and why your financials can't see it yet

What this is

Applied Identities is the company we are building. This page explains the bet behind it.

Companies are handing real work to AI agents faster than they can account for it. Two questions follow, and the market has good answers to neither:

  • Replacement value. An agent takes over work a person used to do. Where did the value go, and how do you prove it.
  • Innovation value. An agent does useful work no one would have paid a person to do, because the agent made it cheap. How do you find that value and count it.

Both kinds are real today and mostly invisible on the books. Value that exists but cannot yet be seen is what we call Dark Value.

Our bet is straightforward. The companies that can prove what their agents are worth will win, and almost none of them can today. We build the tools that make that proof possible. This is about a six-minute read, and there is a model near the end you can run yourself.

Start with the insurers

The clearest sign that AI value is hard to measure came from the industry whose job is measuring risk.

In early 2026, Berkshire Hathaway, Chubb, and Travelers won regulator approval to exclude AI-related damages from standard business insurance. Regulators approved more than four in five of those requests.

The signal is blunt. When the firms that price risk for a living decline to price something, they are saying they cannot yet see it clearly enough to put a number on it. That is the measurement problem in its most expensive form.

The thing the accounts can't see

The blindness has a name and a long history.

The research firm SemiAnalysis calls it Dark Output: real value AI produces that the economic statistics cannot see. When an agent writes a document a lawyer used to bill for, the bill disappears, and the official numbers can show output falling in the exact place where productivity rose. SemiAnalysis sells a product to track this, so we treat their framing as commercial rather than neutral.

The pattern is forty years old. Robert Solow observed in 1987 that the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. Economists later explained the lag: a powerful general-purpose technology demands heavy, invisible investment before its gains appear in the numbers. AI is repeating the pattern at speed.

The value is real. People feel it at their desks. It does not survive the trip to the income statement, because nothing in the deployment was built to make it survive.

That gap is Dark Value, and most companies are creating it and discarding it. The point is short: capability is becoming cheap, and proof is becoming scarce. Scarce things are where value accrues.

How value becomes provable

Proof of an agent's value is not a yes or no. It comes in degrees, and the ladder is clear:

  • A benchmark is a lab claim.
  • A company grading its own agent reads, from outside, like marketing.
  • A deployment a customer can inspect is stronger.
  • An assessment an outside party will sign is stronger still.
  • A risk an insurer will underwrite is strongest, because a disinterested third party has examined the work, priced the failure, and agreed to carry it.

The work itself does not change as it climbs. What changes is who outside the company will stand behind it. This is how independent audit replaced internal say-so after the accounting scandals of the early 2000s. Independence is what makes a claim believable.

The top of this ladder is becoming a live market now. Specialist reinsurers, including Lockton Re with Armilla and Gallagher Re, find that standard insurance was never built for AI risk, that the exposure lands on the company deploying the agent, and that coverage increasingly goes only to firms that can document and prove their controls. The valuable asset is not the policy. It is the verifiable record that makes the policy writable.

The value that compounds

The larger prize is the value no one planned for.

An agent does what it was told to do. It also, at times, does something useful no one specified. That second kind is invisible unless something is watching closely enough to catch it. Caught, verified, and recorded, it becomes value the company can claim.

This is why the asset compounds:

  • A company that measures its agents only against the original plan sees the replacement value, which is roughly fixed.
  • A company that measures continuously sees the replacement value plus the new value its agents keep discovering, and that share grows year over year.

A firm with a year of that record holds something a competitor starting today cannot buy at any price: an accountable history of what its agents are actually worth.

Why this is the company, and the investment

Capability is becoming a commodity. The ability to prove what an agent's work is worth is not. Three forces are converging on it at once:

  • Insurers are repricing AI risk and will soon write coverage only against evidence.
  • Regulators (the EU AI Act, the NIST framework and its 2026 agent extension, ISO/IEC 42001) are asking for the same thing: a specification, ongoing measurement, and outside verification.
  • Enterprises are realizing they cannot bank the value they already feel.

Applied Identities builds the instrument that makes agent value legible, defensible, and ultimately insurable. The investment case is two steps:

  • Our product creates real, recognized, compounding value for the companies that deploy it. That value is the market.
  • We capture a share of it.

The client value is the evidence. Our value follows from it. That is what we are building, and it is what we are inviting you to back.
